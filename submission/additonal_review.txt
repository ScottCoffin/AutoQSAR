### Major changes I would make before submission

1. **Remove the held-out-test leakage from the ensemble analysis and regenerate the affected results.** This is the most important issue. The paper repeatedly describes the framework as “leakage-controlled,” yet the ensemble implementation excludes members using **held-out \(R^2\)** and uses a held-out metric in a correlated-member tie-break. :chatgpt-content-reference{index="1"} The ensemble family then supplies the largest number of wins—16/44—and is central to both the title and major conclusions. :chatgpt-content-reference{index="2"} The Limitations section correctly admits the resulting optimism, but for this journal I would not leave it as a caveat. :chatgpt-content-reference{index="3"} **Change the ensemble filtering/tie-breaking to use training/OOF information only and rerun the ensemble results.** Then the “leakage-controlled” claim becomes much stronger.

2. **Make the “honest model selection” comparison genuinely apples-to-apples.** Right now the test-selected protocol can choose among the full library, whereas CV selection excludes Chemprop, Uni-Mol, MapLight+GNN and fusion because those backends do not emit the requisite CV scores. :chatgpt-content-reference{index="4"} Consequently, the dramatic decline from 35/37 to 25/37 top-ten placements is not purely the cost of avoiding test-set peeking; it is partly the cost of removing several candidate families. The manuscript acknowledges this, but nevertheless presents the difference as the “most practically important number.” :chatgpt-content-reference{index="5"} I would either generate CV/OOF selection scores for **all** major families, or rerun the test-selected comparison using exactly the same candidate set eligible for CV selection. Ideally show both:
   - identical-candidate-set: test-selected vs CV-selected;
   - expanded-library benefit: CV-eligible library vs added pretrained/specialized models.

3. **Add some multi-seed replication before making the central comparative claims.** The manuscript is admirably explicit that every result comes from a single split/seed and that there are no confidence intervals, variance estimates, or significance tests. :chatgpt-content-reference{index="6"} But that caveat substantially weakens a title asserting that model classes “match” one another. I would not necessarily repeat all 837 model-dataset fits five times. A reasonable compromise is a pre-specified reduced experiment on the **22 official TDC ADMET tasks**, five seeds, containing representative conventional ML, corrected ensemble, Uni-Mol, and a working Chemprop baseline. That would let you put uncertainty around the main conclusion while keeping the 44-dataset sweep as the breadth analysis. If you do not add replication, I would replace “match” in the title with a descriptive phrase such as **“show comparable benchmark performance”** and avoid language implying statistical equivalence.

4. **Fix the incomplete Chemprop comparison before positioning this as a broad architecture benchmark.** Four of five Chemprop variants failed on most tasks; all Chemprop variants collectively yielded valid results on only 6/44 datasets. TabPFN was disabled, and two larger Uni-Mol V2 variants yielded no valid results. The manuscript itself calls Chemprop coverage its largest known threat to completeness. :chatgpt-content-reference{index="7"} I would prioritize getting **one canonical, well-configured D-MPNN/Chemprop baseline to run across essentially every applicable dataset** rather than retaining five partially broken variants. Otherwise a reviewer can reasonably argue that differences between “architectures” partly reflect backend reliability rather than predictive performance. Likewise, do not visually present TabPFN as part of the benchmark unless it was actually evaluated.

5. **Delete the “GA negative result.”** Genetic-algorithm tuning was disabled. :chatgpt-content-reference{index="8"} Yet §3.8 is titled “Genetic-algorithm tuning: a negative result” and concludes that model-library breadth rather than evolutionary search drives competitiveness. :chatgpt-content-reference{index="9"} The Conclusion goes further and says “Genetic-algorithm tuning added nothing.” :chatgpt-content-reference{index="10"} You cannot infer that from a procedure that was not run. Either execute an actual GA-vs-fixed comparison, or simply state in Methods that GA capability exists but **was not evaluated in this benchmark**.

   - The title says **45 benchmarks**, but the principal analysis is **44 datasets**: 45 were attempted, with one abandoned. :chatgpt-content-reference{index="11"} :chatgpt-content-reference{index="12"} I would title it around **44 analyzed benchmarks**, not 45 attempted.
   - The family called **“Uni-Mol V1 (3D pretrained)”** includes Uni-Mol V2. :chatgpt-content-reference{index="13"} Call it simply “Uni-Mol (3D pretrained).”

7. **Reframe the leaderboard analysis more conservatively.** You already acknowledge that “estimated ranks” are not actual leaderboard submissions and that 10 of 37 datasets use non-equivalent locally generated splits. :chatgpt-content-reference{index="18"} I would make the **27 benchmark-equivalent datasets**—22 TDC + 5 Polaris—the primary comparison, and rename “estimated leaderboard rank” to something like **“rank among curated published reference values.”** Then present the 10 non-equivalent comparisons separately. The manuscript's underlying story is quite strong, and its unusually candid treatment of failure modes and benchmarking bias is well aligned with this journal.
